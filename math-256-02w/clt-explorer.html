A probability under an assumption

This left-tail probability measures how unusual this result or a lower mean would be if the assumed population model were correct. It is not the probability that the assumed mean is true.

Use a random, representative sample with independent measurements and known population σ. Normality of the sample mean is exact for a normal population and approximate otherwise. The example uses a large-sample approximation; severe skewness, dependence or heavy tails can undermine it. A one-sided direction should be chosen before examining the data.

Population & sample

SE = (σ / √n) × √[(N − n) / (N − 1)]

For a simple random sample without replacement, with σ² = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² / N. If population variance is defined using N − 1 instead, use SE = (S / √n) √(1 − n/N).

Correction factorSelected sample fraction

Boundary checks

At n = 1 the correction is 1. At n = N a census has SE = 0. With replacement, draws are independent and SE = σ / √n, even for a finite population.

The notes recommend applying the correction when n exceeds 5% of N. The correction formula is valid at any permitted fraction without replacement; 5% is a convention for deciding when ignoring it has little effect. It corrects the variance, but does not guarantee a normal sampling distribution.

Course rule (Section 6.4): For the probability methods in these notes, use a normal population or n > 30. For a mean, use σ/√n; for an individual, use σ. The simulation also lets you explore why the n > 30 guideline depends on population shape.
The theorem, assumptions & how to use this explorer

For independent, identically distributed observations with finite mean μ and finite variance σ², the standardized sample mean approaches the standard normal distribution as n increases. The mean of X̄ is μ and its standard deviation is σ/√n under these assumptions, whether or not X̄ is already approximately normal.

A normal population gives an exactly normal sample mean for every n. For other populations, the speed of convergence depends on skewness and tail behavior. “n > 30” is a classroom guideline, not a universal cutoff. Some symmetric populations work well at smaller n; strongly skewed or heavy-tailed populations may need much larger n. Infinite-variance populations are outside this classical CLT.
